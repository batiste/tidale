# Aeropolis — Bribes & Grievances (draft 2)

**Status: parked (2026-10-17).** Playable (play.html, vs bots), but the Spire never found a natural role. See Post-mortem at the end.

Replaces plan-1.txt (kept for history). Prototype: play.html.

Why draft 2: with the Spire heights as points and districts worth −1..2, a purchase never paid back more than it cost:
the best play was to buy rarely, and the Spire was "points spent to buy points". Now the Spire is the purse, the clock
and the multiplier: what you own pays according to where your pawns end.

3–5 players.

## Components

- Spire track 1–20, in 3 tiers: 1–7 ×1, 8–14 ×2, 15–20 ×3.
- Per player: 2 pawns (Citizen, Outcast), betting cards 1, 2, 3.
- 30 districts: 15 High City (7 × 2 shares, 5 × 3, 3 × 4), 15 Low City (7 × 1, 5 × 2, 3 × 3).
  High City is worth more: its multiplier (your Citizen) only goes down.

## Setup

Citizens on 20, Outcasts on 1. Shuffle the districts. Random first auctioneer.

## Auction (one per turn)

1. The auctioneer reveals a district.
2. Everyone plays one betting card face down, or passes. Reveal together.
3. The highest card wins. Between equal cards, the player closest to the auctioneer's left wins
   (ties used to cancel: half the districts burned).
4. Winner: takes the district; their Citizen sinks by their card.
5. Every player who bid and lost: Outcast rises 1.
6. Nobody bids: the district burns, every Outcast rises 1.
7. Every played card goes to its owner's discard. Empty hand: take your discard back.
   (A betting deck of 0, 4, 5 and Veto, drawn by losers, was cut: a drawn 4 or 5 simply won, at a known high price.)
8. The auctioneer token passes left.

## End

- A player's Outcast reaches their Citizen: revolution. The current round is finished, then the game ends.
- Deck empty: the game ends.

## Score

High City shares × your Citizen's tier + Low City shares × your Outcast's tier. Tie: highest Citizen.

So every bid is a bet on where your pawns end: winning sinks your Citizen (your High City is worth less), losing raises
your Outcast (your Low City is worth more). A Low City district bought early pays for every auction you lose after.

## Open questions

- Tiers: 3 bands of 7, or a finer scale? A single step across a band can double a portfolio.
- Losing bids: back to your hand instead of the discard? Then bidding your lowest card is a free rise.
- Does the revolution still need a counterweight for the player who triggers it?

## Build

play.html: cheap hot-seat prototype with simple bots (profiles: Establishment, Revolutionary, Balanced, Closer).

## Post-mortem

**What did not work:**
- The Spire was shoehorned in. It went through three jobs, none of them natural:
  points spent to buy points (draft 1: a purchase never paid back), then purse + clock + multiplier (draft 2),
  with the Outcast's rise justified only as a consolation for losing. "My Citizen lost a bribe, so my Outcast
  gains support" has no story.
- Money = points with small, known district values: buying rarely beat passing; the best play was to avoid auctions.
- Every fix added a patch: majority bonus, betting deck, Veto, 0, tie rules, tiers, uneven shares. The core loop was
  not carrying the game (the same warning as Coup).
- Extra betting cards (0, 4, 5, Veto) made bids obvious: a drawn 4 or 5 simply won, at a known high price.
- Ties that cancel burned half the districts; a hand that only refreshes when empty caused stalls of all-pass auctions.
- The player whose spending ends the game controls the end (rush to the revolution before others build).

**Worth keeping:**
- Secret simultaneous bids with a tiny hand (1, 2, 3, every played card discarded, refresh when empty):
  readable, Knizia-simple, a little card counting. Ties to the player closest to the auctioneer's left.
- The moment that felt right: losing on purpose to lift a multiplier just before the end ("betting now, collecting
  later"). Worth finding a theme where that is natural.
- Two portfolios scored by two different measures (High City by one character, Low City by the other).
- "Revolution finishes the round" as an end condition: nobody ends the game with one bid or one pass.
- Process: a cheap one-file prototype plus a headless harness was enough to see each problem in minutes.

**Lesson for the series:** start from a mechanism that has a story, then see whether the Spire fits it; do not
start from the Spire. If the two characters and the track keep needing justification, the track may belong to
another game.
